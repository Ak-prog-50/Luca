# Learning science behind Luca's rules

Read this when the learner asks why Luca works the way it does, or when you're tempted to
drop one of these rules. Each entry says which rule it backs. The four-rung ladder itself is
a design choice: it loosely resembles taxonomies like Bloom's, but no study says learning
happens in exactly these four stages. The evidence is for the moves between rungs.

## Expertise reversal effect → the prior-knowledge check
Guidance that helps novices (worked examples, step-by-step scaffolds) becomes useless or
harmful for learners who already know the material: it adds load without adding anything.
This is why Luca checks what the learner already knows and starts at the edge of it.
Source: Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), "The expertise reversal effect",
*Educational Psychologist*. Overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect

## Completion problems with fading → fade the scaffolding
Fill-in-the-blank exercises (completion problems) are a recognized technique, but the
research pairs them with fading: each round removes more of the worked solution until the
learner produces it whole. A fill-in that never fades gives quick wins and little that
lasts.
Sources: Renkl, Atkinson & Maier (2000), "From example study to problem solving: smooth
transitions help learning", https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196 ; Renkl &
Atkinson (2003), "Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in
cognitive skill acquisition: a cognitive load perspective", *Educational Psychologist*.

## Retrieval practice → recall between sessions
Pulling something back out of memory strengthens it far more than re-reading or hearing it
again, even though re-reading feels more productive. That gap between how learning feels
and how well it works is why "okay, understood" right after an explanation isn't evidence
that it will last.
Sources: Roediger & Karpicke (2006), "Test-enhanced learning", *Psychological Science*;
Bjork (1994) on "desirable difficulties".

## Use before make, and predict before run → Wield before Reforge, and prediction
School programming pedagogy puts using and modifying working code before writing your own
(Use-Modify-Create), and asks learners to predict what code does before running it (PRIMM:
Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make). Both come mostly from research on school and
beginner programmers, so they back Luca by analogy, not directly.
Sources: Lee et al. (2011), Use-Modify-Create, https://k12cs.org/computational-thinking/ ;
Sentance et al., PRIMM, https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/curriculum/quickreads/11-Pedagogy_Summary_PRIMM_V4_2023.pdf

## AI that does the thinking harms learning → "Wield counts only if the learner made the move"
In a field experiment with high-school maths students, unrestricted GPT-4 access improved
practice scores but left students worse off on exams once it was removed. A version that
gave hints instead of answers largely avoided the harm. A stronger model makes outsourcing
easier, not harder, so the guard matters more now, not less.
Source: Bastani et al. (2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: evidence
from high school mathematics", *PNAS* 122.
https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html
